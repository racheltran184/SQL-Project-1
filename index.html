01 · The brief

Five board questions,
one database.

Urban Eats runs three cafés: Sydney CBD, South Melbourne and Brisbane City. Management wanted orders, bookings, the menu and staff in one system, and answers to five questions the board kept asking. A database can say what happened. The board needs to know where money leaks, who should act and how to tell if the fix worked.

Who asks what

Outlet managersWhat do failed bookings cost us, and which tables should we protect?
MarketingWhich customers are worth keeping, and when do we lose them?
Front of houseWhat should we sell together to lift the ticket?
Head chef & financeWhich dishes earn the money, and where can price move?
OperationsIs labour cost leaking through late starts and padded timesheets?

How to read the numbers

  • ScaleA designed sample: 30 orders over 25 days, 15 bookings and two days of clocked shifts across three outlets. Patterns are directional; the method is built to run on live data.
  • TestsEvery action carries a trial, a stop rule and a measure, so live data decides before money moves.
  • SourcesRevenue comes from payments, margin from each order line and recency from orders. Stored summary fields are checked, not trusted.

From schema
to a decision.

The database is the product: 11 tables that mirror how a café runs, with constraints that stop bad records at the door. The analysis then asks each question at the grain where the money moves: the clock hour, the ticket, the plate and the booking.

Read the SQL: ticket and line checks 
-- Do ticket totals (what was charged) match the line items (what was sold)?
WITH items AS (
  SELECT Order_ID, SUM(Quantity * Unit_Price) AS Item_Total
  FROM ORDER_ITEM
  GROUP BY Order_ID
)
SELECT
  COUNT(*)                                                  AS Completed_Orders,
  SUM(ROUND(o.Total_Amount - i.Item_Total, 2) <> 0)         AS Orders_Not_Reconciled,
  ROUND(SUM(o.Total_Amount), 2)                             AS Ticket_Revenue,
  ROUND(SUM(i.Item_Total), 2)                               AS Item_Revenue,
  ROUND(SUM(ABS(o.Total_Amount - i.Item_Total)), 2)         AS Gross_Gap,
  ROUND(SUM(o.Total_Amount - i.Item_Total), 2)              AS Net_Gap
FROM ORDERS o
JOIN items i ON i.Order_ID = o.Order_ID
WHERE o.Status = 'Completed';

-- Line prices and costs that disagree with the product master, and the product they actually match
SELECT oi.Order_ID, oi.Product_ID, p.Product_Name AS Keyed_As,
       oi.Unit_Price, p.Price AS List_Price, oi.Item_Cost, p.Cost AS List_Cost,
       m.Product_ID AS Matches_ID, m.Product_Name AS Matches_Product
FROM ORDER_ITEM oi
JOIN PRODUCT p ON p.Product_ID = oi.Product_ID
LEFT JOIN PRODUCT m ON m.Price = oi.Unit_Price AND m.Cost = oi.Item_Cost
WHERE oi.Unit_Price <> p.Price OR oi.Item_Cost <> p.Cost
ORDER BY oi.Order_ID;

03 · What the data says

Six findings,
each tied to a decision.

Data window

Orders from 25 August to 18 September 2024; bookings dated 15 to 26 September; clocked shifts on 17 and 18 September.

Money

Revenue from payments. Contribution is price minus cost on each order line, as sold.

Read with care

A designed sample of 30 orders. Each action pairs the pattern with a four-week trial and a stop rule.
01

Evenings are rostered past the last order.

Paid staff hours stay flat from mid-afternoon to close while orders fade. From 8 pm, the outlets carry 13% of paid hours and none of the 30 orders; the only booking from 8 pm is a table for two in Sydney.

Each evening ends hours after its last customer arrives. Brisbane's last order and booking came by 6 pm against a 10:30 pm finish; Melbourne's by 7:30 pm against 11 pm. An evening hour costs $70–105 in wages for a crew of three or four.

So whatAt the menu's 66% gross margin, an evening hour needs $106–160 in sales, five to seven dine-in tickets, to cover its crew. In this sample, nothing after the last booking comes close.
Read the SQL: paid minutes per clock hour 
-- Paid staff minutes in each clock hour against orders and bookings starting in that hour
WITH RECURSIVE hrs AS (
  SELECT 5 AS h UNION ALL SELECT h + 1 FROM hrs WHERE h < 23
),
clocked AS (
  SELECT ss.Staff_ID, sh.Outlet_ID, sh.Shift_Date, s.Hourly_Rate,
         HOUR(ss.Check_In_Time) * 60 + MINUTE(ss.Check_In_Time)   AS in_min,
         HOUR(ss.Check_Out_Time) * 60 + MINUTE(ss.Check_Out_Time) AS out_min
  FROM STAFF_SHIFT ss
  JOIN SHIFT sh ON sh.Shift_ID = ss.Shift_ID
  JOIN STAFF s  ON s.Staff_ID = ss.Staff_ID
),
paid AS (
  SELECT h.h,
         SUM(GREATEST(0, LEAST(c.out_min, h.h * 60 + 60) - GREATEST(c.in_min, h.h * 60))) AS staff_min,
         SUM(GREATEST(0, LEAST(c.out_min, h.h * 60 + 60) - GREATEST(c.in_min, h.h * 60)) * c.Hourly_Rate / 60.0) AS wages
  FROM hrs h CROSS JOIN clocked c
  GROUP BY h.h
),
orders_h AS (
  SELECT HOUR(Order_Time) AS h, COUNT(*) AS orders FROM ORDERS GROUP BY HOUR(Order_Time)
),
bookings_h AS (
  SELECT HOUR(Reservation_Time) AS h, COUNT(*) AS bookings FROM RESERVATION GROUP BY HOUR(Reservation_Time)
)
SELECT p.h AS Hour, ROUND(p.staff_min / 60.0, 2) AS Paid_Staff_Hours, ROUND(p.wages, 2) AS Wages,
       COALESCE(o.orders, 0) AS Orders, COALESCE(b.bookings, 0) AS Bookings
FROM paid p
LEFT JOIN orders_h o   ON o.h = p.h
LEFT JOIN bookings_h b ON b.h = p.h
ORDER BY p.h;

02

Doubles cover the evening, and the overlap is paid twice.

On 18 September, all three managers and all three kitchen hands worked both shifts: 16.3 to 16.4 hours on the clock. A server did the same on 17 and 18 September. In 7 of those 8 doubles, the second clock-in came before the first clock-out, so 80 minutes were paid twice in two days ($36.67).

Attendance is not the leak. Across 45 shifts, paid hours were 0.7% above the roster. Six people started 10 minutes late, and all six still worked a full shift. No one left early.

So whatChasing late starts saves almost nothing. Give the evening its own lead and a short kitchen shift, so no one works 16 hours to cover a quiet night.
Read the SQL: doubles and minutes paid twice 
-- Staff on two shifts the same day: total paid hours and minutes paid twice
WITH day_shifts AS (
  SELECT ss.Staff_ID, sh.Shift_Date, ss.Check_In_Time, ss.Check_Out_Time, ss.Hours_Worked,
         LAG(ss.Check_Out_Time) OVER (PARTITION BY ss.Staff_ID, sh.Shift_Date ORDER BY ss.Check_In_Time) AS prev_out
  FROM STAFF_SHIFT ss
  JOIN SHIFT sh ON sh.Shift_ID = ss.Shift_ID
)
SELECT CONCAT(s.First_Name, ' ', s.Last_Name) AS Staff, s.Role, d.Shift_Date,
       SUM(d.Hours_Worked) AS Paid_Hours, COUNT(*) AS Shifts,
       SUM(GREATEST(0, TIMESTAMPDIFF(MINUTE, d.Check_In_Time, d.prev_out))) AS Minutes_Paid_Twice,
       ROUND(SUM(GREATEST(0, TIMESTAMPDIFF(MINUTE, d.Check_In_Time, d.prev_out))) * s.Hourly_Rate / 60.0, 2) AS Cost_Paid_Twice
FROM day_shifts d
JOIN STAFF s ON s.Staff_ID = d.Staff_ID
GROUP BY s.Staff_ID, s.First_Name, s.Last_Name, s.Role, s.Hourly_Rate, d.Shift_Date
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1
ORDER BY d.Shift_Date, Paid_Hours DESC;

-- Paid hours against rostered hours, and how often people start late
SELECT COUNT(*)                                                                     AS Staff_Shifts,
       ROUND(SUM(ss.Hours_Worked), 2)                                               AS Paid_Hours,
       ROUND(SUM(TIME_TO_SEC(TIMEDIFF(sh.End_Time, sh.Start_Time))) / 3600.0, 2)      AS Rostered_Hours,
       SUM(TIMESTAMPDIFF(MINUTE, TIMESTAMP(sh.Shift_Date, sh.Start_Time), ss.Check_In_Time) > 5)   AS Starts_Over_5_Min_Late,
       SUM(TIMESTAMPDIFF(MINUTE, ss.Check_Out_Time, TIMESTAMP(sh.Shift_Date, sh.End_Time)) > 5)    AS Leaves_Over_5_Min_Early
FROM STAFF_SHIFT ss
JOIN SHIFT sh ON sh.Shift_ID = ss.Shift_ID;

MySQL 8 · LAG() compares each clock-in with the same person's previous clock-out that day

03

Takeaway leaves the drink behind.

Dine-in and delivery customers add a drink to most food orders: only 2 of 11 dine-in and 1 of 6 delivery food orders had none. At the takeaway counter, 6 of 9 food orders left without one.

Takeaway is already the smallest ticket: $14.88 against $23.22 for dine-in and $25.12 for delivery. A drink adds $4.67 on average, $3.33 of it margin. The one repeated pairing, cappuccino with bruschetta, appears in just 2 orders: a test, not yet a menu section.

So whatThis is a counter habit, not a menu gap. A drink prompt on takeaway food orders targets the one channel where the drink goes missing; every 10 drinks added is $47 of sales and $33 of margin.
Read the SQL: drink attach by channel 
-- Food orders that leave without a drink, by channel
WITH tagged AS (
  SELECT o.Order_ID, o.Order_Type, o.Total_Amount,
         MAX(pc.Category_Name IN ('Hot Beverages', 'Cold Beverages')) AS has_drink,
         MAX(pc.Category_Name NOT IN ('Hot Beverages', 'Cold Beverages')) AS has_food
  FROM ORDERS o
  JOIN ORDER_ITEM oi      ON oi.Order_ID = o.Order_ID
  JOIN PRODUCT p          ON p.Product_ID = oi.Product_ID
  JOIN PRODUCT_CATEGORY pc ON pc.Category_ID = p.Category_ID
  WHERE o.Status = 'Completed'
  GROUP BY o.Order_ID, o.Order_Type, o.Total_Amount
)
SELECT Order_Type,
       SUM(has_food)                                AS Food_Orders,
       SUM(has_food AND NOT has_drink)              AS Food_No_Drink,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(has_food AND NOT has_drink) / SUM(has_food), 1) AS Pct_No_Drink,
       ROUND(AVG(CASE WHEN has_food AND has_drink THEN Total_Amount END), 2)     AS AOV_With_Drink,
       ROUND(AVG(CASE WHEN has_food AND NOT has_drink THEN Total_Amount END), 2) AS AOV_No_Drink
FROM tagged
GROUP BY Order_Type
ORDER BY Order_Type;

MySQL 8 · each ticket is tagged once for food and once for drink before the channels are compared

04

Mains earn the dollars, at the highest food cost.

A main earns $7.70 to $13.70 a plate; a coffee or pastry about $3. Contribution, not margin percentage, pays the rent: Fish and Chips has the lowest margin on the menu (59.8%) and the highest contribution per plate ($13.70).

Fish and Chips, the Gourmet Burger and the Big Breakfast run at 40% food cost, the highest on the menu. A $1 rise adds $1 a plate and pays for itself unless sales fall by more than 6.8% to 8.1%.

So whatTest price where a dollar moves the most: the three 40% food-cost mains. Keep each rise only where volume holds within its break-even.
Read the SQL: contribution per plate 
-- Contribution margin per item from the line itself (price and cost at the time of sale),
-- with mis-keyed lines re-pointed to the product whose price and cost they carry
WITH lines AS (
  SELECT oi.Order_ID,
         COALESCE(m.Product_ID, oi.Product_ID) AS Product_ID,
         oi.Quantity, oi.Unit_Price, oi.Item_Cost
  FROM ORDER_ITEM oi
  JOIN ORDERS o   ON o.Order_ID = oi.Order_ID AND o.Status = 'Completed'
  JOIN PRODUCT p  ON p.Product_ID = oi.Product_ID
  LEFT JOIN PRODUCT m
         ON (oi.Unit_Price <> p.Price OR oi.Item_Cost <> p.Cost)
        AND m.Price = oi.Unit_Price AND m.Cost = oi.Item_Cost
)
SELECT p.Product_Name, pc.Category_Name,
       SUM(l.Quantity)                                         AS Units,
       ROUND(SUM(l.Quantity * l.Unit_Price), 2)                AS Revenue,
       ROUND(SUM(l.Quantity * (l.Unit_Price - l.Item_Cost)), 2) AS Contribution,
       ROUND(AVG(l.Unit_Price - l.Item_Cost), 2)               AS CM_Per_Unit,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(l.Quantity * l.Item_Cost) / SUM(l.Quantity * l.Unit_Price), 1) AS Food_Cost_Pct
FROM lines l
JOIN PRODUCT p           ON p.Product_ID = l.Product_ID
JOIN PRODUCT_CATEGORY pc ON pc.Category_ID = p.Category_ID
GROUP BY p.Product_ID, p.Product_Name, pc.Category_Name
ORDER BY Contribution DESC;

MySQL 8 · margin comes from the price and cost stored on each line, not today's menu prices

05

Big tables hold the seats, and outcomes go unrecorded.

Four of 15 bookings are parties of five to eight, and they hold 25 of 56 booked seats (45%). Every one is an occasion: a business lunch, a birthday, a corporate event and an anniversary.

Outcomes are not closed out. Of 10 bookings whose time has passed, 5 still read "Confirmed", so the show rate can't be measured. Of the 5 with an outcome, 3 were seated, 1 was a no-show and 1 was cancelled.

So whatProtect the seats that matter: a card guarantee and a set-menu offer on parties of five or more. Then record every outcome by the end of service, so no-shows are counted rather than estimated.
Read the SQL: seats by party size and outcomes 
-- Seats by party size and occasion
SELECT CASE WHEN Party_Size >= 5 THEN '5 or more' WHEN Party_Size >= 3 THEN '3 to 4' ELSE '2' END AS Party,
       COUNT(*) AS Bookings, SUM(Party_Size) AS Seats,
       SUM(Special_Requests IS NOT NULL) AS With_Request
FROM RESERVATION
GROUP BY CASE WHEN Party_Size >= 5 THEN '5 or more' WHEN Party_Size >= 3 THEN '3 to 4' ELSE '2' END
ORDER BY MIN(Party_Size) DESC;

SELECT Status,
       SUM(TIMESTAMP(Reservation_Date, Reservation_Time) <  TIMESTAMP('2024-09-21 16:20:00')) AS Past,
       SUM(TIMESTAMP(Reservation_Date, Reservation_Time) >= TIMESTAMP('2024-09-21 16:20:00')) AS Upcoming,
       SUM(Party_Size) AS Seats,
       ROUND(AVG(TIMESTAMPDIFF(MINUTE, Booked_Datetime, TIMESTAMP(Reservation_Date, Reservation_Time))) / 1440, 1) AS Avg_Lead_Days
FROM RESERVATION
GROUP BY Status;

MySQL 8 · "past" means before the latest timestamp in the records (21 September 2024, 4:20 pm)

06

Repeat guests return within two weeks.

Seven of 20 buyers ordered twice, and every one came back within 15 days of their first order; the median gap was 11 days.

Of the 13 one-order customers, 2 have gone more than 15 days without a second order. Three more have a first order in progress, including the host of a seated corporate party of six, and 2 registered without buying. The stored last-visit date was older than the last order for 19 of 20 buyers, so recency is computed from orders.

So whatTime the nudge to the habit: day 10 after a first order, before the window closes. A fixed 30-day "at risk" rule fires after the second visit would normally have happened.
Read the SQL: days to a second order 
-- Days between a customer's first and second completed order
WITH seq AS (
  SELECT Customer_ID, Order_Date,
         LAG(Order_Date) OVER (PARTITION BY Customer_ID ORDER BY Order_Date, Order_Time) AS prev_date
  FROM ORDERS
  WHERE Status = 'Completed'
)
SELECT Customer_ID, prev_date AS First_Order, Order_Date AS Second_Order, DATEDIFF(Order_Date, prev_date) AS Gap_Days
FROM seq
WHERE prev_date IS NOT NULL
ORDER BY Gap_Days;

04 · The next 90 days

One problem, one owner,
one test.

Each action names the trigger, the scope and a stop rule. Sizes come from the sample; dollar effects are arithmetic, not forecasts.

Every shift is a fixed 8-hour block. Fit the roster to when customers come, and the doubles go too.

01

Close two hours after the last booking slot

Do

For four weeks, close Brisbane at 8 pm and Melbourne at 9:30 pm. Sydney keeps 10 pm for its 8 pm tables.

Why

Brisbane's last order and booking came by 6 pm, Melbourne's by 7:30 pm. The trial frees four evening hours a night: $280–420 in wages.

Rule

Reopen any hour that averages $106–160 in sales, enough to cover its crew.

TrackSales per labour hour after 7 pm
02

Give the evening a lead and a short kitchen shift

Do

Managers work one shift. A senior barista leads the evening, and a 4 pm-to-close kitchen shift replaces the double.

Why

On 18 September, all three managers and all three kitchen hands worked 16.3 to 16.4 hours.

TrackDays over 12 hours on the clock (target: none)
03

Block overlapping clock-ins

Do

The time clock refuses a second clock-in until the first shift is closed, and holds any day over 12 hours for approval.

Why

80 minutes were paid twice in two days ($36.67), and the overlap recurs with almost every double.

05 · Strategy for the next year

Fit labour to demand,
then grow the ticket.

The 90-day tests show what works. The strategy builds it into how Urban Eats runs: rosters drawn from demand, a ticket that sells the drink, guests won back inside their own habit and numbers that reconcile every night. Four evening hours a night are worth $280–420 in wages; every 10 takeaway drinks add $33 of margin.
